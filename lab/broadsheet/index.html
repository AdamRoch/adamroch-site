An essay in five sections on the web's scarcest resource

Attention is a Material.

Every feed is a mining operation, and the ore is your morning. Five short sections on the scarcest material online, what it costs, and how to stop paying it out by the scoop.

Scroll to declassify ↓

§ 01 Scarcity Same allotment for everyone

A commodity with no warehouse

Gold is rare. Uranium is rarer. But both hold still — you can stockpile them, insure them, steal them. Attention won't hold still. It arrives at roughly sixteen waking hours a day, unbuffered, and whatever you fail to spend today is simply gone.

That makes it stranger than anything with a ticker symbol. It cannot be mined faster, warehoused, or shipped to a market with higher prices. Everyone gets the same allotment, and no logistics network on earth can move an hour of attention from someone who squanders it to someone who needs it.

Scarcity like that deserves respect. Instead, we built machines to strip-mine it.

§ 02 Extraction Skinner, with push notifications

The machinery of the take

The extraction equipment has names you know. Infinite scroll removes the stopping cue. Autoplay removes the decision. Pull-to-refresh is a slot lever, and the variable-reward schedule behind it predates the web by decades — B. F. Skinner ran the original trials; he just didn't have push notifications.

None of this is subtle once you look. An ad-funded web is priced in attention, so every incentive bends toward taking more of it: more pages per visit, more minutes per session, more sessions per day. The metric was never what you got out of the internet today. It is what it got out of you.

§ 03 Compounding Residue or evaporation

Wherever it points, things grow

Here is where the mining metaphor breaks down, in our favor: attention is not only consumed. Wherever you point it, things grow.

An hour in the feed evaporates. The same hour spent building, writing, or reading something hard leaves residue — skill, memory, a half-formed idea that shows up uninvited on Thursday. Invested attention compounds like interest. Harvested attention depreciates like inventory.

So "is this app good?" is the wrong question. The right one: when I hand this my evening, what does the evening become?

§ 04 Craft Endings are a feature

Spend, don't harvest

Designers say engagement the way casinos say time on floor. The honest word is spending. A page either spends the reader's attention on something worth owning, or it harvests it for someone else's numbers.

So design like the reader paid at the door, because they did — in the one currency that never refunds. Cut the autoplay. End the article. Let a finished page feel finished; endings are a feature.

This page runs on the same bet: one column, no rail, no infinite anything. When you reach the bottom, it lets you go.

§ 05 Ledger Unexamined things are expensive

Keep your own books

You cannot rebalance an economy overnight, but you can audit yourself. For one week, write down where the hours actually went. Most people find the figures embarrassing, in the specific way unexamined things always are.

Then treat attention like the material it is: finite, priced, and yours. Spend it on work that compounds, on people who notice, on rest that actually rests. The web will happily offer a hundred ways to fritter it before breakfast.

Let it keep its ore. You were never the mine.

How it's printed

This page is a love letter to flat: paper ground, black ink, one spot color, hairlines everywhere. No WebGL, no gradients — the fidelity lives entirely in typesetting and motion curves. The masthead breathes on Clash Display's weight axis (a variable font, wght 200–700), tweened through a proxy object into font-variation-settings so the ink visibly swells as each line rises out of its mask.

The redaction bars are absolutely positioned rectangles of ink, peeled away by a scrubbed ScrollTrigger with transform-origin on the right. The pull quotes pin for 130% of the viewport, then scatter letter-by-letter with scroll velocity: SplitText cuts them into characters, each gets a random direction vector, and velocity drives how hard they fling apart. Stop scrolling and they settle back into a readable sentence. 

// velocity scatter: fast scrolls fling the type apart
const kick = gsap.utils.clamp(-1, 1, self.getVelocity() / 1800);
chars.forEach((c, i) => gsap.to(c, {
  x: dir[i].x * kick * 96,
  y: dir[i].y * kick * 60,
  rotation: dir[i].r * kick,
  duration: 0.55, ease: 'power3.out', overwrite: 'auto',
}));

Plate I is Canvas 2D: about fifty vertical rules sampled at twenty-six points each, displaced by a gaussian falloff around the eased pointer position, with every sixth rule pulled in spot orange. It only runs its rAF loop while intersecting the viewport.

prefers-reduced-motion collapses everything to final state — bars hidden, type at rest weight, plate rendered once, pins removed. On mobile the scenes unpin and the type rescales; the essay reads fully either way.
